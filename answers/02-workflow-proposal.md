## Who actually uses this — distinct from who's buying it

The six discovery-call stakeholders are mostly **not** the product's day-to-day users. Worth stating explicitly, since it's easy to conflate "who we're pitching to" with "who the UI has to work for," and they're different people with different needs:

**Primary persona — the reviewing analyst.** Modeled directly on the APAC analyst's own words, generalized across the 24-person team. A regional indirect-tax analyst embedded in one of the 3 hubs, comfortable in SAP and spreadsheets but not technical — she is the one who will actually operate FR3/FR3a/FR9's review-and-confirm workflow, transaction by transaction, all day.
- **Today, without this tool:** manually determines tax treatment, chases suppliers for compliant invoices ("most of my month"), works across SAP + the shared mailbox + spreadsheets. Her own name is effectively on what gets filed — the risk is personal, not abstract, which is why the Head of Tax Risk's "wrong matters more than slow" lands on her specifically.
- **Skepticism baseline, stated in her own words:** "no model fixes that" (re: dirty supplier master data) — she has already, unprompted, identified a failure mode a naive tool would miss. **Design implication: the tool has to visibly earn trust by showing its work, not ask for blind trust in a score.** A confidence number with no evidence attached is worse than no tool at all for a user who already expects it to be wrong in a specific, nameable way.
- **What she needs from the UI (ties directly to FR2, FR3, FR3a, FR9):** fast batch-confirm for the ~75-80% of routine, resolvable-from-invoice-plus-PO cases (`research/assumed-customer-profile.md` §2's invoice-mix estimate); for the ~20-25% that get routed, evidence and reasoning presented plainly enough to make her own judgment quickly — not a black box, and not a wall of raw data she has to re-derive the answer from herself. For the duplicate-vendor case specifically, she needs to see *all three* conflicting vendor records side by side (FR2a) — the exact evidence that would let her do in seconds what currently costs her real time to notice at all.
- **What would make her reject it, regardless of accuracy:** a tool that silently resolves a conflict she'd have caught herself, or one where verifying its output takes longer than just doing the work manually.

**Secondary persona — the hub tax manager.** Modeled on the EMEA regional manager. Oversees a team of analysts like the one above; personally reviews the highest-stakes exceptions but is not the primary daily operator. Cares less about any single transaction and more about aggregate signal: is the supplier-master-data problem she named actually shrinking (FR11's measured-scale reporting), is her team's exception rate trending the direction it should. **Success for her, specifically, is her own complaint coming back to her as a real, tracked number** (`case-facts.md` §8) — not a vague reassurance that "the AI is handling it."

**Not users — stakeholders the product must satisfy without ever putting a UI in front of them:**
- **VP, Global Indirect Tax** — consumes the burndown report (FR10), not the review queue.
- **Head of Tax Risk** — the veto-holder; earns trust through the eval framework and precision metrics (`prototype/evals/eval-plan.md`), not through hands-on use. Likely wants periodic audit-trail access, not a daily login.
- **Head of Tax Technology, Enterprise Security** — technical/compliance gatekeepers on architecture and the security review; neither touches the product's functional surface at all.

**Why this distinction matters for the pitch, not just the design doc:** the deck is presented to the non-users (VP, Head of Tax Risk), but the workflow requirements (FR2, FR3, FR3a, FR9) have to be defended on the reviewing analyst's terms, since she's the one whose actual daily behavior determines whether the precision/recall numbers in section 6 below hold up in practice. A design that satisfies the buyer's story but not the analyst's daily reality is the fastest way to a technically-compliant system nobody actually uses well.

## The reviewing analyst's assumed workflow — current state, then proposed

### Current state (assumed, before this tool exists)

1. **Posting happens in real time, with a silent risk baked in.** An AP invoice lands in one of the 3 SAP instances via MIRO (PO-matched) or FB60 (direct/non-PO invoices). The tax code applied is pulled from whatever vendor master record the invoice is coded against. **This is the exact moment a duplicate-vendor conflict becomes a wrong tax code — with nothing in the posting flow checking for it.**
2. **The three-way match (PO / goods receipt / invoice) is a real gate, but it isn't a tax gate.** It checks quantity, price, and terms; mismatches get blocked for manual resolution. A transaction can pass the three-way match cleanly (right quantity, right price) while carrying the wrong tax code entirely — the match and the tax-treatment question are checked independently, and only one of them is actively gated at posting time.
3. **Recoverability review is a separate, later, batched pass — this is where she actually does her named job.** Rather than being checked at posting, whether a transaction is *recoverable* and correctly *reported* gets reviewed on a periodic (monthly/quarterly, filing-cycle-aligned) cadence, using vendor line-item reports to reconcile what already posted weeks earlier against documentation completeness. **This is the structural reason "wrong" isn't caught until well after the transaction happened** — the review that would catch it runs on a delay by design, not by accident.
4. **Chasing suppliers is manual, mailbox-and-spreadsheet driven, with no dedicated tooling.** When she finds a non-compliant invoice during that batched review, she emails the supplier and tracks the back-and-forth herself — consistent with her own "most of my month" framing, and with industry data suggesting AP teams spend a large share of time on exactly this kind of discrepancy resolution.
5. **Duplicate-vendor conflicts are essentially invisible in the normal cycle.** SAP's own duplicate detection is address/match-code fuzzy-matching *within one instance* — there's no cross-instance identity concept, and cleanup is normally a periodic, manual project, not something triggered by any single transaction. In practice, a duplicate-vendor tax-profile conflict across Northgate's 3 instances likely surfaces only when something already went wrong downstream (an audit question, a supplier dispute) — not proactively.
6. **No urgency signal exists anywhere in this flow.** Nothing in a standard SAP-native process tracks "how close is this specific transaction's reclaim window to closing" — if it's tracked at all, it's a separate, easy-to-neglect spreadsheet.

**Net effect of the current state:** the only real check on tax correctness happens well after posting, on a delay, with no visibility into which duplicate-vendor conflicts or near-expiration transactions are silently sitting in the gap between posting and that delayed review.

### Proposed workflow, with the system (maps each gap above to an existing requirement)

1. **Posting in SAP is unchanged** — read-only, no write-back (FR7/NFR4). The system observes what already exists; it doesn't touch the posting flow itself.
2. **The determination runs immediately after posting is visible, not weeks later at cycle-end.** FR1/FR2 produce the three-part determination and a confidence score on the same cadence the backlog-and-stream pipeline operates (FR8) — closing gap #3 above: the check that used to run on a batched delay now runs close to real time.
3. **Entity resolution catches the duplicate-vendor conflict at the moment it would otherwise become invisible.** FR2a/DR2 flag a conflicting tax profile across resolved vendor records immediately, with all conflicting records shown as evidence — directly closing gap #5 (duplicate conflicts as reactive/rare) and gap #1 (the silent risk at posting).
4. **The format-compliance check runs as its own explicit step, naming the specific problem.** FR6 replaces "she notices something's off during her batch review" with "the system names the missing field" (e.g., the German *Leistungsdatum* trap) — she still has to chase the supplier herself (EX1 keeps outreach out of scope, an honest half-solution worth naming as such), but she now knows immediately and specifically why, instead of discovering it in a slow monthly pass.
5. **The queue is urgency-weighted, not just confidence-weighted.** FR5 surfaces the transactions closest to losing their reclaim window first, closing gap #6 — the thing the current process has no mechanism for at all.
6. **Routine, high-confidence cases get a fast batch-confirm; flagged cases get full evidence up front.** FR3/FR3a/FR9 replace "review everything at the same depth, on a delay" with "confirm the routine ~75-80% quickly, investigate the ~20-25% that actually need it, with the evidence already assembled" (`research/assumed-customer-profile.md` §2's invoice-mix estimate).
7. **The aggregate picture closes the loop for her manager, not just her.** FR10/FR11 turn the EMEA manager's own complaint into a tracked, reported number, per the persona work above.

**What this journey does not claim to fix:** the actual supplier-chasing communication (EX1) and the underlying dirty-data problem itself (the duplicate vendor records still exist in SAP — the system flags them, it doesn't clean them) — worth stating plainly rather than implying the tool makes the master-data problem disappear.
1. The agent(s) and the workflow stages, end to end.
2. Where exactly a human stays in the loop, and why there specifically — not "a human reviews it somewhere."
3. Who/what owns write-back to SAP or filing, since the agent never can (read-only, no writes, any version).
4. **The exception queue is urgency-weighted, not confidence-only.** A low-confidence case with years of reclaim-window runway can wait for review; a high-confidence case with weeks of runway left (per the time-to-expiration attribute in `answers/03-information-model.md`) needs to jump the queue regardless of confidence — otherwise it expires while sitting in a review pile that was prioritized the wrong way. See `case-facts.md` §4 for why this isn't hypothetical: Italy and India's windows can be single-digit months for a late-in-year invoice.
5. **One pipeline, two inputs — not two systems.** The same ingest → 3-part determination → confidence score → urgency-weighted exception routing → human review pipeline runs against the accumulated backlog (Phase 1, "urgent recoverables") and against the ongoing monthly stream (Phase 2+) — the input changes (batch backlog vs. continuous flow) and which axis dominates the queue sort changes (urgency dominates on backlog, since some of it is genuinely about to expire; confidence dominates on fresh volume, since it's just starting its full window), but it's the same engine. Phase 1 isn't a throwaway cleanup tool — it's the platform's first deployment, pointed at its hardest, highest-value data first. Caveat to have ready: backlog data is likely "colder" (older records, staff turnover, weaker evidence trails), so expect a higher exception rate on backlog than the system will settle at once run on fresh, ongoing volume — don't present Phase 1's exception rate as steady-state.

6. **What the model optimizes for, in tax terms — this needs to be plain-English content in the pitch, not just an internal design choice.**

Confusion matrix, defined for this specific determination (does the transaction flag as recoverable):
- **True positive** — flagged recoverable, genuinely is. Captured correctly.
- **False positive** — flagged recoverable, actually isn't. If auto-claimed, this is an incorrect filing: the exact audit-exposure/penalty risk behind the $4M figure.
- **False negative** — not flagged, but actually was recoverable. Contributes to the $8-30M left-on-the-table problem.
- **True negative** — not flagged, genuinely not recoverable. Correctly ignored.

Precision = TP/(TP+FP): of what's flagged, how much is right. Recall = TP/(TP+FN): of what's actually recoverable, how much gets caught. **Accuracy is the wrong headline metric here** — most transactions are probably "correctly taxed, nothing special," so a model that flags almost nothing would score high on accuracy while providing zero value and zero protection.

**The optimization target isn't one number for the whole system:**
- **Auto-processed determinations (no case-by-case manual re-derivation of the judgment before the analyst acts on it): optimize for precision.** A false positive here is exactly the Head of Tax Risk's stated fear — "wrong matters more than slow" means tolerating lower recall (missing some auto-processable cases) rather than letting a bad auto-claim through.
- **Routing (what gets sent to a human vs. ignored): optimize for recall.** Never let the system quietly decide "not recoverable" with confidence it doesn't have — when uncertain, route to a human rather than defaulting to no. This protects the $8-30M opportunity without creating audit risk, because nothing gets filed without a human acting on it.
- **The confidence threshold is the actual dial between these** — raising it increases auto-processing precision and sends more to detailed human review; this is why the threshold tightens over time as accuracy is demonstrated (already in the plan), rather than being fixed on day one.

**Human-in-the-loop, stated for the pitch, not just the architecture — and this is a hard constraint, not a design choice:** the system cannot write to SAP or any filing system (FR7; the brief's own "read access to SAP, no writes, any version"), so it never files anything itself, at any confidence level. Its output is always a recommendation surfaced in its own UI, never an executed action. What confidence changes is how much scrutiny the analyst applies before they go act on it in SAP themselves, using their own existing access — a high-confidence item still requires the analyst to key it in, just as a fast batch-confirm-and-file rather than a from-scratch investigation; a low-confidence item requires them to work the case first. A false positive only becomes a real filing if the model is wrong *and* the analyst files it anyway — a second, independent check, not a formality. Don't describe this as the system "posting," "auto-filing," or "executing" anything, even for high-confidence cases — it recommends, the analyst always acts. This sentence belongs in the pitch.

**On a current-error baseline to beat:** the brief gives costs of errors ($4M penalties, $8-30M unrecovered), not rates — computing an actual error rate needs a transaction-volume denominator the brief doesn't provide. A rough, heavily-stacked-assumption estimate is possible (see `claims.md`) but too wide to defend as a number. The credible move: **establish the current human-only process's actual precision/recall empirically in Phase 1** (sample past determinations, re-review them) — this becomes both the benchmark the model has to beat and the starting point for the burndown reporting in `answers/05-roadmap-pricing.md`.

7. **A second, distinct capability: invoice format-compliance checking — confirmed in Phase 1/EMEA scope.** The APAC analyst's complaint ("the invoice does not meet the local format rules, not because anyone coded it wrong," `answers/01-diagnosis.md`) points at a failure layer the tax-judgment engine doesn't touch at all — the *document* fails a jurisdiction's mandatory-field checklist before any recoverability judgment is even relevant. Research confirmed this is a real, present-day EMEA problem, not just a theoretical one: EU VAT-recovery firms name it as a recurring live rejection cause, Germany has a concrete named formal-field trap (missing *Leistungsdatum*), and Italy/Poland/Spain are actively layering national e-invoicing *clearance* mandates on top of the EU's Art. 226 content minimum — an expanding, not static, source of compliance risk. This is a checklist/schema problem, not a judgment problem — lower-risk than the recoverability determination itself, and it directly feeds that determination (a non-compliant invoice can't be reliably scored regardless of what it says). Confirmed in scope for Phase 1, not deferred to an APAC phase.

**Two real gaps this surfaced in the assumed EMEA footprint and the roadmap sequencing, name explicitly rather than assume away:**
- Switzerland and Turkey, both in the assumed EMEA country list, are not EU members and aren't covered by Art. 226 at all — a compliance check built only against the EU baseline would silently miss them. Needs its own rule set if those two are in the pilot's actual scope, or an explicit note that they're excluded from the compliance-check's v1 coverage.
- Singapore's rules are the same shape as the EU's (checklist + threshold + single-field rejection) and should generalize cheaply. India's IRP/IRN clearance step is structurally closer to Italy/Poland's emerging model, and China's state-issued fapiao system is fundamentally different in kind — not a config change, real rework. Both should be named as explicit later-phase risks in the roadmap (`answers/05-roadmap-pricing.md`), not silently assumed to extend for free when the jurisdiction-level sequencing reaches them.

**Automated supplier outreach — explicitly out of scope.** Drafting, sending, and tracking corrected-invoice requests to suppliers is a different kind of system (supplier communication/workflow automation, touching the shared mailbox as a data source) than "determine the right tax treatment." It would help the APAC analyst's specific time burden, but it's a separate build outside the brief's stated ask and the 2-engineer/8-week budget. This is the answer to the prototype's required "one thing chosen not to build, and why" talking point (`answers/04-prototype-notes.md`) — the compliance *check* stays in scope, the outreach *workflow* doesn't.

8. **Where the model actually runs, resolved: "determine externally" (outside SAP) and "nothing leaves our tenant" (outside Northgate's cloud boundary) are answering two different questions, not contradicting each other.** State this explicitly on the assumptions slide rather than leaving it implicit.

**Recommendation, hyperscaler confirmed as Azure for planning purposes:** the determination layer's model calls run against **Azure OpenAI Service, provisioned inside Northgate's own Azure subscription**, private-endpoint-only, no-training-use terms. AWS Bedrock (via PrivateLink) is kept as a one-line noted alternative in case the brief's curveball ever touches this assumption — not presented as a hedged parallel option.

All three are reached exclusively through a private endpoint with public network access disabled, no-training-use terms in place. Buildable by 2 engineers in 8 weeks (API integration, not infrastructure operation), capable of the multilingual extraction the prototype needs, and a real match for "nothing leaves our tenant": the resource lives in Northgate's subscription, data is pinned to their chosen region, never used for training, never visible to the model provider, never transits the public internet.

**Who pays for it:** because the resource lives in Northgate's own subscription, Northgate's cloud provider bills Northgate directly for compute/API consumption — this is separate from, and not included in, Ampliwork's engagement fee (`answers/05-roadmap-pricing.md`). Standard for a bring-your-own-cloud deployment; it also means Ampliwork's fixed fee doesn't need to absorb Northgate's own usage-scaling infrastructure cost.

**Two options considered and declined, say so if asked:**
- **A public multi-tenant model API** — fast, but directly disqualified by the security stakeholder's line.
- **Self-hosting an open-weight model** (Llama, Mistral) — the most literal answer to "nothing leaves our tenant," and worth naming as evidence of having considered it, but not realistic here: ~15-20% added ops overhead, GPU/MLOps burden neither engineer is scoped for, and no fine-tuning budget in 8 weeks.

**One precision point to state directly, not gloss over:** "nothing leaves our tenant" can't mean literally zero third-party infrastructure ever touches the data — even a customer-subscription Azure OpenAI resource runs on Microsoft-operated shared compute underneath the contractual/logical isolation boundary. The defensible, checkable definition to propose: data stays within the customer-owned resource and region, is never used for training, is never visible to the model provider, and never transits the public internet. That's an answer a security review can validate against evidence (architecture diagrams, subprocessor disclosure, network config, DPA terms) — a better answer in the room than asserting an absolute no version of this achieves short of air-gapped on-prem hardware.

9. **"On-premise" and "tenant-isolated cloud" are not the same claim — don't let them get conflated in the room.** On-premise means physical hardware Northgate owns and operates, zero cloud provider involved; that's the self-hosting option above, considered and declined. Tenant-isolated cloud (the actual recommendation) is still Microsoft's hardware — the isolation is which subscription controls the resource, which region data is pinned to, and the contractual no-training-use terms, not physical ownership.

**What "everything stays in-tenant" implies architecturally, beyond the model call:** NFR1 says "all data processed," not "the model call specifically" — so the constraint covers the whole application, not just the LLM piece. This has two consequences worth stating explicitly:
- **Single-tenant-per-customer deployment, not multi-tenant SaaS.** A normal SaaS vendor runs one central service serving every customer, logically separated by customer ID. That's impossible here — it would mean every customer's data flows through Ampliwork's own infrastructure, exactly what "nothing leaves the tenant" forbids. The whole stack deploys once *per customer*, inside *their* subscription. This is also why the ~11-week security review isn't a one-time cost the way it would be for a normal SaaS vendor (one central review, every customer relies on the same SOC 2 report) — each customer's security team reviews *their own* instance. The only way this scales as a product rather than staying a bespoke service per account is a reusable trust package that makes each subsequent review faster (`answers/06-productization.md`, hypothesis H3) — the review itself doesn't go away.
- **Two components, two different constraints, worth separating explicitly (`prototype/README.md` has the code-level version of this):**
  - **The determination/reasoning layer** (LLM calls: extraction, entity-resolution check, compliance check, tax determination) — must be a private-endpoint-only resource inside Northgate's subscription. Decomposed per capability, not one monolithic prompt, matching how the eval framework already scores these independently (`prototype/evals/eval-plan.md`) — and the natural integration pattern is retrieval (pull the relevant knowledge-base entries for a transaction's jurisdiction/entity into the prompt as context) rather than fine-tuning, which there's no budget for.
  - **The review UI/app layer** (the queue, evidence display, confirm/override, knowledge-base review, reporting) — mostly conventional CRUD engineering, no LLM in the loop for most interactions, invoked only at determination trigger-points. Must *also* run inside Northgate's subscription, since it touches real transaction data too — a UI hosted anywhere convenient because "it's not the AI part" is a real, easy-to-make mistake, not a hypothetical one.

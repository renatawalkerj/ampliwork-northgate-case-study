## 1. VAT/GST/sales-and-use tax mechanics

**The three-part decision Northgate's analysts make on every invoice** (matches the brief): (a) was tax charged correctly, (b) is it recoverable, (c) where should it be reported. This is standard — indirect tax recovery hinges on the invoice being both *substantively* valid (real supply, real business use) and *formally* compliant (right fields, right format for the jurisdiction).

**Reclaim windows — the brief's "typically three to four years" is directionally right but hides real jurisdictional spread:**

| Jurisdiction | Input VAT/GST deduction or reclaim window | Source |
|---|---|---|
| UK | 4 years from the end of the year the VAT was incurred | Marosa VAT, UK Statute of Limitations manual |
| Germany | 4 years (general limitation period) | VATupdate country guides |
| Netherlands | 5 years from end of the calendar year | Marosa VAT Netherlands manual; VATupdate NL guide |
| France | Much shorter in practice — roughly 2 years for reclaiming/deducting previously-missed VAT | Cyplom "VAT prescription in France" |
| EU cross-border refund (13th/8th Directive mechanism) | Standard deadline is June 30 of the following year; some member states (Belgium, Denmark, Estonia, Greece, Hungary, Italy, Latvia, Poland, Portugal, Romania, Spain) extend to 9 months into the second year | Avalara EU VAT recovery guide; Marosa "EU VAT refund" |
| US sales/use tax refunds | Most states: 3 years from filing/payment; some 4 years (e.g., Arizona, from due date or filing date, whichever later); a few states use 6 months from date of payment as an alternate window (e.g., California) | TaxValet state-by-state statute of limitations guide; Commenda; CDTFA Sales & Use Tax Annotations |
| Singapore GST | 5 years from the end of the relevant accounting period. Plus a Singapore-specific wrinkle: if input tax is claimed before the supplier is paid, payment is due within 12 months or the claim must be repaid to IRAS (reclaimable again within 5 years once paid) | IRAS (Singapore's tax authority) directly — "Claim Input Tax in the Correct Accounting Period," "Paying suppliers after claiming GST" |

**Takeaway for the pitch:** "three to four years" is a reasonable average to say out loud, but it is not one clock — France is much tighter (~2 years), the Netherlands looser (5 years), and the window's *start date* (calendar year end vs. payment date vs. filing date) varies by country. A system that treats the reclaim window as a single global constant will misfire in exactly the countries where it matters most. This is a legitimate "what breaks if you get the information model wrong" point for the case.

**EU VAT invoice compliance requirements** (this is the invoice-compliance rule set most likely to be Northgate's binding constraint given its European hub in Rotterdam):
- EU VAT Directive 2006/112/EC, **Article 226**, sets a harmonized and *exhaustive* list of up to 15 mandatory invoice fields — issue date, sequential invoice number, supplier and customer VAT ID numbers, full name/address of both parties, quantity/nature of goods or services, taxable amount, VAT rate/amount, and a few others. Member states cannot demand fields beyond Articles 226/227/230. (VATupdate, "EU VAT Directive 2006/112/EC explained: Art. 226")
- Courts (CJEU) have held that the *right* to deduct is a substantive right that in principle shouldn't be denied for purely formal invoice defects if the tax authority can otherwise verify the substantive conditions were met — but in practice, tax authorities routinely deny claims administratively when the paper trail is incomplete, and taxpayers then have to litigate or supply secondary evidence (contracts, POs, goods receipts, correspondence) to win it back. (VATupdate roundup of ECJ Article 226 case law; VAT IT "Why are input VAT claims rejected?")
- Most commonly cited real-world rejection triggers: missing/invalid supplier VAT registration number, VAT amount on the invoice not matching what was actually charged, wrong or missing address, missing date. (VAT IT; Fonoa "Compliance Risks of Invalid VAT & Tax IDs")

**Read for the case:** this directly corroborates the APAC analyst's quote in the brief ("a lot of what we cannot recover is because the invoice does not meet the local format rules, not because anyone coded it wrong") — invoice-compliance rejection is a well-documented, real mechanism, not an excuse.

## 2. What actually drives indirect-tax recovery leakage — the load-bearing question

**Bottom line: there is no single, credible, quantified public benchmark that cleanly splits leakage into "% caused by misclassification" vs. "% caused by dirty supplier master data" vs. "% caused by non-compliant invoices."** This is a real gap — don't force a number that isn't there. What exists instead is convergent qualitative commentary from multiple credible sources, all naming the same three failure modes without ranking them:

- **KPMG's 2026 Indirect Tax Benchmarking Survey** ("Benchmarking Indirect Tax in a data-driven era") frames **poor data quality, legacy ERP systems/manual workarounds, and gaps in skills and ownership** as the dominant pressures multinational indirect-tax functions report — data quality named first and most often in KPMG's own framing. (kpmg.com/xx/en/our-insights/operations/indirect-tax-benchmark-survey.html) I could not pull a specific "% of respondents cite X as top cause" figure from the public page or the linked 2021 PDF (the PDF didn't extract cleanly) — flagging this as something worth trying to get the full report for if there's time before Thursday.
- **Deloitte** (in e-invoicing/digital indirect-tax commentary): calls out **incorrect customer/supplier master data, missing or incorrect tax registration numbers, and imprecise product/service classification** as "long-standing inconsistencies that legacy VAT processes could absorb" but that break down under digital/e-invoicing scrutiny — i.e., Deloitte's own framing puts master-data quality and classification in the same sentence, not one over the other. (deloitte.com, "Managing Compliance Disputes as Indirect Tax Goes Digital"; related Deloitte indirect-tax recovery material)
- **Vendor commentary (Oversight.com, Vertex)** independently flags **duplicate/fragmented vendor master records** (the same supplier existing multiple times with different tax profiles — exactly the EMEA regional manager's quote in the brief) as a named, recognized driver of VAT leakage and audit risk, separate from coding/classification errors. (Oversight.com "Five Ways Duplicates in Your Vendor Master Create Risk"; Vertex "Master Data: The Key to Real-Time E-Invoicing Excellence")
- **Invoice-compliance-specific rejection** is documented as a mechanism (see Section 1) but no source quantifies it as a *share* of total leakage versus the other two causes.

**Read for the case:** all three voices in the brief (VP: coding/data-entry; EMEA manager: dirty master data; APAC analyst: invoice compliance) are each independently corroborated by outside sources as *real, named* failure modes in the indirect-tax literature. None is dismissed by the research, and none is crowned "the" answer by any credible benchmark. That itself is useful: the honest position to take into the case is "all three are documented and none is provably dominant industry-wide — Northgate's own transaction-level exception data, not an outside benchmark, has to settle which dominates *for them*." That's a legitimate, defensible stance rather than a gap to paper over — and it argues for a phase 1 that's diagnostic (surface which failure mode is actually driving Northgate's number) rather than a phase 1 that bets the design on one theory.

## 4. Does EU/EMEA actually have this problem in practice, or does the mechanism just exist on paper?

Fast pass, run to answer one question before finalizing EMEA-pilot scope: is invoice-format-compliance failure a real, present-day EU problem for a multi-country filer, or only a CJEU-case-law abstraction?

**Bottom line: real and documented, not just theoretical — though the only *quantified* breakdown found is vendor-sourced and should be treated with the same caution as the Concur "54%" and Avalara "€5B" figures already flagged in Section 3.**

- **Advisory-firm commentary working actual EMEA cases** names this as a live, recurring failure mode, not a hypothetical: incomplete invoices, missing supplier VAT numbers, and documentation "not meeting local formatting requirements" are cited as recurring reasons cross-border EU VAT reclaims fail, by a firm whose actual business is filing these reclaims for multinationals. (VAT IT, "Why are input VAT claims rejected?", vatit.com)
- **A vendor-published "2024 audit data" rejection breakdown** (Lovat Compliance): missing/wrong supplier tax number 28%, expense category not deductible in destination state 19%, missing certificate of taxable status 14%, late submission 11%, illegible invoice scan 9%, wrong claimant name 8%, other technical issues 11%. **Treat as ASSUMED** — no named underlying study, sample, or methodology; the vendor sells VAT recovery services. But directionally notable: a formal/paperwork defect (wrong tax ID) is named as the single largest rejection cause in this breakdown, ahead of a substantive issue (ineligible expense category). (vatcompliance.co / Lovat Compliance, "VAT Recovery Procedures for International Companies")
- **A real, country-specific example, not a generic one:** German VAT audits commonly flag a missing *Leistungsdatum* (date of supply, distinct from the invoice issue date) as the single most common finding — a field that sits inside Art. 226's "date of supply" requirement but is routinely missed because it's not the same as the invoice date most templates capture. This is exactly the kind of jurisdiction-specific formatting trap a generic invoice template walks into. (VATupdate commentary on German VAT audit practice)
- **A structural, EU-specific, and worsening trend — not a static baseline:** several member states in Northgate's assumed EMEA footprint are layering national e-invoicing *clearance* mandates on top of the harmonized Art. 226 content minimum. Italy's SDI/FatturaPA has been mandatory for domestic B2B since 2019 (live today); Poland's KSeF becomes mandatory in stages from February–April 2026; Spain's Verifactu/B2B e-invoicing mandate has been pushed to January/July 2027. In a clearance model, the tax authority's own platform validates and stamps the invoice before it is legally valid — so an invoice with all 15 Art. 226 fields correct can still be rejected on pure schema/format grounds that have nothing to do with whether the underlying transaction or tax treatment was right. This mechanism is actively expanding across the EU right now, which argues against treating "invoice compliance" as a fixed, masterable checklist. (EDICOM, "Poland will make B2B electronic invoicing mandatory"; general clearance-vs-network model overviews, VATupdate)
- **A genuine gap the case's own EMEA list creates:** two of the countries in Northgate's assumed EMEA footprint — Switzerland and Turkey — are not EU member states, so Art. 226 does not apply to them at all; each runs its own separate national invoice-content regime. A compliance pre-check built only against the EU Art. 226 baseline would silently fail to cover roughly a fifth of the EMEA country list. Worth surfacing as an information-model boundary, not just a research footnote — it's exactly the kind of thing that "breaks if you get this wrong."

**Recommendation for Part 1:** this clears the bar. EU/EMEA-specific evidence — an advisory firm working real EMEA reclaim cases, a concrete country-specific audit-finding example (Germany), and an accelerating wave of national e-invoicing clearance mandates that are actively raising the formal-compliance bar (Italy live, Poland imminent, Spain queued) — is enough on its own to justify format-compliance pre-checks as legitimate Phase 1/EMEA-pilot scope. It does not need APAC evidence to carry the case; APAC corroborates rather than originates the argument (see Section 5).

## 5. APAC mandatory invoice-content requirements (Part 2)

Same depth as the Section 1 EU research. Singapore first (Northgate's APAC hub), then India and China.

### Singapore — IRAS, direct primary source

IRAS requires a standard tax invoice, issued by a GST-registered supplier, to carry (per IRAS's own "Invoicing Customers" guidance, iras.gov.sg):
- The words **"Tax Invoice"** shown prominently on the document
- Supplier's name, address, and **GST registration number**
- Invoice date
- A unique, sequential invoice number
- Customer's name and address
- Description of the goods or services supplied, and quantity
- Total amount payable **excluding** GST, shown separately
- The **GST rate** applied
- Total GST payable, shown separately
- Total amount payable **including** GST

A **simplified tax invoice** is permitted only where the total amount payable including GST does not exceed **S$1,000** — above that, the full tax invoice is required. Tax invoices must be addressed to the claiming GST-registered business itself (not an employee or third party) to support an input tax claim, and must be retained for at least **5 years**. Missing even one mandatory field gives IRAS grounds to treat the document as non-compliant and disallow the associated input tax claim. (IRAS, "Invoicing Customers," iras.gov.sg; IRAS e-Tax Guide, "Adopting GST InvoiceNow Requirement")

**Read for the case:** this directly matches the pattern already established for the EU (Section 1) — a defined, checkable mandatory-field list, a threshold below which a lighter-weight document suffices, and single-field omission being sufficient grounds for rejection. Singapore is also mid-rollout on **InvoiceNow** (its Peppol-based e-invoicing network) — a format-standardization move parallel to what's happening in the EU, reinforcing that this isn't a EU-only phenomenon.

### India — CBIC / CGST Rules, direct primary source (with a caveat)

Mandatory tax invoice content is set by **Rule 46 of the CGST Rules, 2017** (Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs). The rule enumerates roughly 19 lettered particulars (a)–(s), including: supplier's name, address and GSTIN; a serial number (max 16 characters, unique per financial year); date of issue; recipient's name, address and GSTIN (or Unique Identity Number if registered; additional detail required for unregistered recipients above ₹50,000); HSN/SAC code; description of goods or services; quantity and unit; total and taxable value; applicable tax rate(s) and amount by tax head (CGST/SGST/IGST/UTGST/cess); place of supply (with state name/code for inter-state supply); delivery address if different; a reverse-charge indicator; signature or digital signature of the supplier or authorized representative; and, where applicable, a QR code carrying the Invoice Reference Number (IRN). (CBIC, Rule 46 of the CGST Rules 2017, taxinformation.cbic.gov.in — full rule text mirrored via ClearTax, "CGST Rules: Chapter VI: Tax Invoice, Credit and Debit Notes," where the primary CBIC domain itself couldn't be fetched directly in this pass due to a TLS certificate error)

On top of the content rule, **e-invoicing is now mandatory** for GST-registered entities above a turnover threshold — currently ₹5 crore (about US$600K) annual aggregate turnover — requiring the invoice to be reported to a government Invoice Registration Portal (IRP) in a prescribed ~50-field JSON schema (Form GST INV-01) before it is valid; the IRP returns a unique **IRN** and signed QR code that must appear on the final invoice. Entities with turnover ≥ ₹10 crore must report within 30 days of invoice date (effective April 1, 2025). Without a valid IRN on an in-scope invoice, input tax credit is at risk of denial. (ClearTax, "What is e-Invoicing Under GST?"; Tally Solutions, "E-Invoice in GST" — both citing the GSTN/CBIC e-invoicing framework; not independently verified against the GSTN portal directly in this pass)

**Read for the case:** India is structurally the closest APAC analogue to where the EU is heading — a government clearance/validation step (IRP) sitting on top of a detailed mandatory-content rule (Rule 46), functionally identical in shape to Italy's SDI or Poland's KSeF. If Northgate's India entity is in scope for any phase, the "compliance pre-check" problem there is arguably *harder* than the EU's, since a missing/wrong IRN is a harder rejection than a missing field on a paper invoice.

### China — State Taxation Administration (fapiao / e-fapiao), secondary sourcing only

China's system is structurally different from both the EU and India: input VAT credit requires a **special VAT fapiao** (as opposed to the ordinary/general fapiao, which does not support input credit), and the fapiao is only valid once verified and recorded in the State Taxation Administration's (STA) invoicing system — the invoice itself is government-issued/numbered rather than supplier-generated-and-validated after the fact. Mandatory content includes a unique invoice code and 20-digit number issued by STA, seller and buyer tax ID numbers, invoice date, description of goods/services, applicable tax rate and VAT amount, and total amount in CNY. A fully digitalized **e-fapiao** rolled out nationwide for B2B, B2G, and B2C transactions effective **December 1, 2024**. Only VAT "general taxpayers" (not small-scale taxpayers) can issue/use special VAT fapiao for input credit purposes. (Secondary sources only in this pass — China-Briefing/Dezan Shira, MSA Advisory, PTL Group, and VATupdate's China e-invoicing briefing, all describing STA rules; **I could not directly fetch or verify against chinatax.gov.cn (the STA's own site) in this pass — language and access barriers** — so treat the specific field list as ASSUMED pending a primary-source check, though the overall shape — fapiao required, STA-verified, e-fapiao now mandatory nationwide — is corroborated across multiple independent secondary sources.)

**Read for the case:** China's model is the most different from the EU pattern of the three — the invoice isn't "compliant or non-compliant" against a checklist so much as "issued by the state system or it doesn't count for input credit at all." A generic "invoice format pre-check" designed around the EU's field-checklist logic would need real rework, not just a config change, to extend to China. That's a genericization risk worth flagging explicitly if China is ever discussed as a later-phase market, even though this fast pass didn't reach primary-source confidence there.

## Combined recommendation — does this belong in Phase 1/EMEA scope?

**Yes — the EU/EMEA evidence alone is enough to put invoice-format-compliance pre-checks in Phase 1/EMEA-pilot scope.** It is not a case of "the mechanism only shows up once you look at APAC." Section 4 shows real EMEA-specific documentation (advisory-firm case experience, a concrete country-level audit-finding pattern in Germany, and a live/expanding wave of national e-invoicing clearance mandates in Italy, Poland, and Spain) that stands on its own. Building the pilot in EMEA first is well-supported by the evidence, not just organizationally convenient.

APAC does **not** need to wait for its own phase to be *acknowledged* — Singapore's IRAS rules are structurally the same shape as the EU's (checklist + threshold + single-field rejection), so a well-designed information model built for EMEA should generalize to Singapore with field-list swaps, not a redesign. But two things should explicitly stay out of Phase 1 and be named as such rather than silently assumed-away: (1) India's IRP/IRN clearance step, which is closer to a live EU concern (Poland/Italy) than to Singapore's simpler model, and (2) China's fundamentally different state-issued-fapiao model, which likely cannot be handled by the same information model at all and should be flagged as a "known won't work as-is" rather than deferred quietly.
